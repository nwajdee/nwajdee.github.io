The lipstick hypothesis: The study that demystified human attention

Fifty men, one hundred faces, and an eye-tracker. The seven-second result that explains why your best post died in the feed.

Jordan Nwachukwu Marketer · creative technologist · Pixelhd Studios, Lagos

A few years ago, two university researchers in Manchester ran a simple eye-tracking study. Fifty male test subjects watched a slideshow of one hundred women's faces. Half wore light makeup; half wore none. The researchers wanted one number: which part of a face holds attention longest?

Eyes: 0.95 seconds. Hair: 0.85 seconds. Lips wearing red lipstick: seven seconds. Seven times longer than the eyes. Nobody argued with the data.

What the study actually proves

Not that red lipstick sells. That we do not pay equal attention to all stimuli. Some inputs command attention by design; most are filtered out before you consciously see them. The filter has a name: the Reticular Activating System, or RAS - your brain's internal security officer. It reviews incoming sensory information and decides what is important enough to reach your attention and what gets ignored. It filters roughly 99% of what arrives.

Here is the bad news for anyone publishing content: your post is incoming sensory information. If RAS classifies it as predictable, routine or repetitive, it never reaches consciousness. It is not that people rejected your content. They never saw it.

Focus creates blindness. - Mike Murdock

Attention is a currency, and it has a capacity

Notice the verbs we use. Lend me your ears. Pay attention. Shakespeare put a financial term in Mark Antony's mouth because the instinct is primal: attention is something we transact with, exchange for value. The global ad industry spent $493 billion in 2016 trying to buy it. Your audience's capacity for it is measured in seconds - about eight, by the estimate this book works with - and roughly 93% of published content never gets even that.

Which means attention has two phases, and they need different strategies. Voluntary attention is a choice: you consciously focus on something interesting, and RAS keeps redirecting you to what you've shown interest in. Involuntary attention is automatic: your focus is snatched by whatever is most compelling in view. The decision has already been made for you. Content that wins starts in phase two.

The hack underneath the lipstick

Red lips worked because they disrupted a pattern. A face is a predictable arrangement; a saturated red mouth in a monochrome field is not. RAS monitors for patterns and ignores them - and alarms at anything out of place, unexpected, or wrong for the context, no matter how trivial. That is hack one: the pattern interrupt. Your content cannot stand out by fitting in. Zag when the world zigs.

The other six hacks - novelty, contrast, emotion, nostalgia, questions, humor - are the same move in different costumes. Each one exploits a documented flaw in the security officer. And once you have the seven seconds, you have about eight more to turn momentary attention into focus. The rule for that is simpler than it sounds: be interesting but incomplete. Curiosity is attention held over time.
